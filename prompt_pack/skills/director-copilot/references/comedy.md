# Comedy: staging, camera and timing for laughs

For sitcoms and comic scenes. Read with [film](film.md), [camera](camera.md) and
[performance](performance.md). Everything here comes from how comedy is shot and cut on screen,
translated into what a beat can ask H3 for. None of it is render-proven yet; see
[status](status.md) before a piece depends on it.

## The joke lives in the reaction

- **Plan the reaction shot before the line.** In a sitcom the biggest laugh is usually the face
  that hears the line, not the mouth that says it. For every punchline, decide whose face takes it
  and give that face its own shot or its own window.
- **Hold after the punchline.** The beat after the joke is part of the joke: the reactor's face
  stays on screen until the reaction peaks and starts to fade, about 1-2 s, then the cut. One
  beat for a normal laugh, two when the reaction *is* the joke. Write it as a held moment with a
  sound ("for one breath nobody moves, the fridge humming"), never as empty time with no face
  state, or someone talks into it.
- **A beat is a small physical action, not dead air:** glasses come off, a can stops halfway to
  the mouth, a head turns slowly. Give every pause one.
- **The take.** A person sees or hears something and the face changes once: a blink, a frozen
  smile, eyes widening. The **double take**: a glance, a look away, then the head snapping back,
  eyes wide. The **slow burn**: a face held still while the anger rises, jaw tightening, then one
  line. Write each as the exact moves in order, at medium or close size.
- **A failed reaction shows its tell:** the listener's mouth moves through the line, or the brows
  act out the plot. Keep the face nearly still, eyes on the source of the joke (off the frame if
  need be), and hold: the cut does the work, and the same still face reads differently after
  different shots. Mugging harder is not funnier.
- **Close-ups for comedy stop at the shoulders.** Tighter feels invasive and kills the laugh;
  keep big comic faces at medium close.

## Coverage, like a multi-camera sitcom

- **One wide to show the room, singles for the lines, singles for the reactions.** The wide comes
  back whenever a sight gag needs room: a sight gag shot too close is no gag.
- **Everyone faces the open side of the set.** Sitcom sets have no fourth wall: people stand along
  the counter or sit on the couch facing the cameras, and play the scene three-quarter toward
  them. With plates, that is the plate's side of the room (continuity.md).
- **Matched pairs.** Two singles cut against each other are the same size, or the cut feels off.
  Their eyelines oppose (one looks screen-left, the other screen-right) at the same height; both
  looking the same way is the tell of a crossed line.
- **A two-shot keeps both at a similar size with the gap between them visible.** One sharp face
  and a blurred shoulder is over-the-shoulder coverage, not a two-shot.
- **A three-shot stands them in a triangle, one a little out of line** (the odd one out, the one
  who is about to be the joke). The miss is a two-shot that crops the third person.
- **Look space:** the extra room in the frame goes on the side the person looks toward. Crushing
  it (the face near the edge it looks at) is for someone trapped, on purpose.
- **Entrances land on a line or business that pulls focus** ("hi", a door bang, a prop held up),
  never a silent slip-in, and the arrival is one take from the door to the mark (status.md).
- **Talk on the move.** A walk across the set gets a line or business on the way; give the room a
  reason for the route (the coffee machine on the side you want them to end up).

## Gags made with the frame

- **Things entering the frame** at the worst moment: a head popping up from below the counter, a
  hand reaching in from the edge with the wrong thing. Name the edge it comes from and that it
  stops.
- **People leaving the frame** in a way that says something: walking out mid-sentence, the other
  person still talking to the empty spot.
- **There and back again:** someone walks over to see what something is, sees it, and walks
  straight back to where they were. The return is the joke; it needs its real seconds.
- **The reveal by camera move:** the camera arcs or pulls back and what is now in frame changes
  the meaning (the empty seat, the mess, the person who heard everything). One move, a named end.
- **The timed sound:** a single sound on the punchline (a can hiss, a stool creak, a bell, a
  chime). Write it on the moment; it lands about half a second early.
- **The lighting cue:** a light snaps off or flares on a line. Use rarely.
- **Rule of three:** a pattern set up twice and broken the third time (two near-misses, the third
  person says it outright). Put the three in different shots so the pattern reads.
- **Matched transitions:** cut from one scene to the next on a matching shape, movement or sound.
- **The smash cut:** a confident claim, then a hard cut straight to it going wrong. Do it in the
  edit or as a restart that opens on the result. Change at least two things across the cut
  (sound and shot size, or tone and time); two sides with the same brightness, size and mood
  do not smash.
- **The crash-zoom sting:** on the punchline or the shock, the camera stays put and the lens snaps
  in on the reactor's face in under a second, then holds dead still. Once or twice an episode.
- **The pre-lap:** the next sound starts before the picture changes (the whinny outside, then the
  heads turn to the window). A sound that lands only with the cut reads flat.

## Rhythm

- **Change of tempo gets laughs:** a fast exchange, then one line delivered slowly; a list where
  the last item is said differently.
- **Callbacks:** an early line, prop or sound comes back near the end with a new meaning. Plant it
  where the viewer can see it (SKILL.md, *plant what pays off*).
- **The button:** every scene ends on one last small laugh, a line or a look, held 2-3 s before
  the cut (`tail: hold` on a film's last hop).
- **Escalate:** each turn of the scene is worse than the one before, then the reversal, then the
  button.

## In H3 terms

- **Fewer cuts, better cuts.** Cut for the line or the reaction; move the camera for everything
  else. A continuous action or a reveal stays in one shot (status.md).
- **Timestamps mark cuts and spoken lines.** A straight action is written as ordered prose.
- **Every face in frame has a state on every line:** the listener reacting, the background busy.
- **Sound bed in every hop** (the room tone), plus the one comic sound on the joke.
- **Pace with lines, not silence.** Long gaps between turns played tame. Fill them with quick
  reactions and replies, each new line in its speaker's own shot: lines dropped into a walk, an
  action stretch or a shared wide came out early and from the wrong face. Keep only the silences
  that are the joke (a freeze, a sting's hold, a silent exit).
- **Restage a joke, never cut it to make a shot safe.** A gag that broke in one form usually has
  another: a stamp moved below the frame lost its laugh; the same stamp in a two-shot with no line
  in it kept the joke and the fix. When a joke has to go, say so and say how it could come back.
- A laugh track is added in the edit, not prompted.
